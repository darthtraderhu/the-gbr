A website proposal usually looks like this: a price, a deadline and a screenshot of the future home page. The picture looks good. You say yes, and months later you find out what you got.

**The problem isn't the picture. It's that you can't buy anything from a picture.** It doesn't show what happens on the second click, on mobile, in the basket or after the quote request.

## What a picture doesn't show

In the audits of recent months, nearly all the costliest problems were on the buyer's journey, not in the design.

**The "Online tickets" menu item showed offer cards, but no basket.** Anyone who came to buy couldn't. On a home page screenshot, that menu item looked perfect.

**Gift vouchers by e-mail only, paid by bank transfer, delivered by post.** People buy a gift in the evening, on their phone, right away. Three steps turned into ten.

**Payment by bank transfer only.** Some buyers stop at this step.

**A quote request after which nothing happens.** The enquiry lands in a mailbox, and from then on it depends on who looks at it and when.

None of these is a design flaw. Each one is about **how a buyer gets from interest to payment, and who deals with them along the way.** That's what needs designing, not just the home page.

## What a good proposal includes

Three things are worth asking for before you say yes to anyone.

**1. A design that explains itself.** Not just a picture, but the typefaces, the colours with measured contrast, the home page, an inner page and the mobile view. Before and after, side by side, so you see what changes and why.

**2. A system plan, not just a page plan.** Where do leads come from? Where does the enquiry land? Who handles it, and how fast? What do we measure to know whether it works? **A website is one part of sales, and it's only worth something when it connects to the rest.**

**3. Something you can click through.** A prototype with your own catalogue, your prices and your copy on it, not made-up products. That way you find out before the decision what's missing, what's unnecessary, and what you hadn't thought of.

## Why it pays off for you

Because the cheapest change is the one you catch before building starts.

If a clickable plan shows that a buyer needs ten steps instead of three to reach payment, it can be redrawn in an afternoon. If the same thing turns up after launch, it's another round of development.

There's one more benefit: **the plan also shows what doesn't need replacing.** Sometimes the current shop engine can stay, and it just needs a better front end. Sometimes sorting out one or two processes brings in more than a whole new site.
