If someone asks how many pages your website has, you probably count the menu. Home, services, about, references, contact.

**Google doesn't look at the menu.** It looks at what it finds at your address: whatever your sitemap lists, whatever links point to, and whatever it has ever found. That list is often longer than you'd think.

On the websites we audited in recent months, we kept finding three kinds of "invisible" pages.

## 1. Left behind by the template

A website rarely starts from a blank page. It's built from a template or from a copy of an earlier project, and the template brings its own sample pages.

On one service company's site, **two pages showed an electrician's sample copy in full**, with lorem ipsum filler. They couldn't be reached from the menu, but they were in the sitemap, which means the company itself was asking Google to look at them.

On another site, the placeholders were still in the product descriptions: "X years of warranty", "Mon–Fri from X to Y", "LINK HERE". Someone started filling them in, then something more urgent came up.

And almost everywhere we find the content management system's default first post, "Hello World!", which nobody deleted after installation.

## 2. Left behind by another company

This is rarer, but more awkward. On the home page of a technical repair service, among the "Featured" services, **there were two photography offers**: portrait shoots and outdoor shoots. The company doesn't do photography.

The explanation is probably simple: the developer started from an earlier job, and these two blocks stayed in. A visitor doesn't think of the explanation, though. They think **nobody has checked this site.** And if nobody checked the site, what else hasn't been checked?

## 3. Put there by someone else

This is the most serious case. On one company site we found **four posts in four languages, with links to gambling sites.** They didn't appear in the blog listing, so the company's staff couldn't have seen them. They were in the sitemap, though, set to be indexed.

That isn't a typo or a template leftover. It means **someone got access to the site and is using the company's name and address to promote their own business.** On two separate dates, so nobody noticed after the first time either.

This can have several consequences:

- Google may show a warning next to the site in search results,
- the company's name gets linked to searches it has nothing to do with,
- and if someone got in once, the gap is probably still open.

## Why nobody notices

Because these pages weren't made for the company's eyes. They're not in the menu, they don't show up on the blog page, and rarely does anyone scroll through every post in the content management system.

**Your site works. It just says more than you mean it to.** No error message, no complaint, until one day someone asks.

## How to check it yourself

You can check three things in ten minutes, without technical knowledge.

**Open the sitemap.** Type `your-domain.com/sitemap.xml` into the browser. If you get a list, go through it: do you recognise every address? If not, open it and see what's there.

**Search for your own site.** In Google, type `site:your-domain.com`. This shows which of your pages Google knows about. Page through the results and watch for anything in a foreign language, unfamiliar or oddly titled.

**Read the home page with a stranger's eyes.** Go through it as if you were seeing it for the first time. Is every block about you? Does every link work? Is every number and claim still true today?

If you find something on any of these, the first question is **who has access to the site's admin panel.** We wrote about that in more detail in [our article on company accounts](/en/writing/who-can-still-log-in).
