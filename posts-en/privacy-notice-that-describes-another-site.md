At most companies the privacy notice gets written once. Someone drafts it, or adapts it from a template, it goes into the footer, and nobody touches it again.

**The website doesn't stand still.** A map gets embedded, a video, a new form, a newsletter sign-up. An old tracking script gets removed. The hosting moves.

A few years later, the notice describes a website that no longer exists.

## Two reviews, one pattern

Over the past few weeks we reviewed two company websites from the outside, looking at legal, accessibility, search and security signals. Two industries, two developers, two different systems.

**The privacy notice told the same story on both.**

### What it describes, but the site doesn't do

One notice described warranty registration, a personal page for registered users, remarketing cookies and analytics tracking in detail.

**None of it was running on the site.** No registration, no analytics, no remarketing. Maybe it existed once, or was planned, or came over from someone else's template.

On the other site, one paragraph named an entirely different company: **the template's placeholder company name had been left in**, and nobody had noticed. It also mentioned a joint controller without saying who that was.

### What the site does, but it doesn't describe

This is the more serious direction, because it affects every visitor.

One site loaded its fonts from Google's servers on every page view. The contact page had an embedded map, the video page had twenty embedded players, all loading immediately. **The visitor's IP address reached a third party before they had a chance to choose anything.**

None of this appeared in the notice. Neither did the hosting provider, or the bot-protection service on the forms.

On the other site, the notice covered neither the analytics that was actually running nor the newsletter offered at registration.

## What the date alone gives away

One notice was dated 2020 and quoted registration numbers from a national data-protection register that hasn't been maintained since May 2018. **It didn't mention the GDPR anywhere.** And it said visitors give their consent "by using the website".

The other had no date and no version number. In that case the company itself can't say which version was in force on a given day.

**Whether, and how much, any of this is a legal problem is for a lawyer to say, not us.** We record what we see. But you don't need to be a lawyer to see this: if a document describes things that aren't there and leaves out things that are, it isn't describing the website the visitor is using.

## Small things that add up

The reviews turned up several more discrepancies, each one minor on its own:

- **Three different URLs for the same notice**, one of which, the one the cookie banner linked to, returned an error.
- **The forms called it by a different name** than the document's own title.
- **Two different tax numbers and two phone numbers** for the same company, depending on which page you looked at.
- **A single checkbox** covering both the quote request and the newsletter, with no link to the notice.
- **A reject button only on the cookie banner's second layer.** The first screen offered two choices: "Accept" and "Customise".

None of this is dramatic. None of it takes the site down. **That's exactly why it stays there for years.**

## Why the document always falls behind

It isn't negligence. It's structural.

**The website is changed by the developer or the marketing team. The notice was written by a lawyer.** Between the two, nobody's job is to speak up when something is added or removed.

Embedding a video takes two minutes. Nobody thinks the legal text should change because of it. Removing a tracking script takes even less. And the notice stays as it was.

**So rewriting the text once isn't the fix.** Six months later you're back where you started.

## What to check now

If you have half an hour, anyone can run through this on their own site:

1. **What loads for a new visitor before they click anything?** Maps, videos, fonts, chat, tracking scripts. Each of them sends data somewhere.
2. **Are they in the notice?** And the other way round: is there anything in it that's no longer on the site?
3. **Does it have a date and a version number?**
4. **Is it at a single URL?** And do all the links to it, in the footer, the cookie banner and the forms, point there?
5. **Do the company details match** across the notice, the imprint and the contact page?

If you're unsure about any of these, have a lawyer look at it.
