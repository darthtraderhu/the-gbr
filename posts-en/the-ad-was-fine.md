We recently went through the results of a B2B campaign. Industrial product, a big-ticket purchase, the buyer a company director. The question was why the ads hadn't delivered what was expected of them.

We went through the ad account. **The targeting was sensible. The copy was fine. The spend was proportionate.**

Then we clicked the ad and looked at what happened next.

## First question: how would we know?

The website ran no measurement at all. No analytics, no ad platform tracking code.

The ad's link had no campaign tags either: the few parameters at the end of a URL that later show which campaign a visitor came from. **Even if there had been analytics, this traffic couldn't have been separated out.**

Facebook appends a unique ID to every click so the website can report back what became of the visitor. It was there on every link. **Nothing was reading it.**

Which means at the end of the campaign **nobody could say how many quote requests it produced.** Maybe none. Maybe ten. There was no way to tell.

## Why this is more than a missing number

Many people think of measurement as a report at the end of a campaign. Nice to have, but the ads run without it.

**That's not how modern ad platforms work.** Measurement isn't for the report. The ad itself needs it.

If the platform can see which click turned into a quote request, it can go and find people like them. You can optimise the campaign for that. Retargeting and lookalike audiences are built from it. It tells you what a lead costs.

**Without measurement, none of this works.** All the platform knows is that someone clicked. So it optimises for clicks, and goes looking for people who click a lot. That isn't the same as people who buy.

For this campaign we went through it point by point: of the ad platform's ten key capabilities, **it could actually use two.**

## Second question: what does the visitor see?

This was the more surprising part.

On the landing page, the offer was **a single image.** The copy, the product, the terms and the phone number, all baked into the picture.

On a desktop you don't notice. On a phone:

- **The text in the image was tiny.** And it couldn't be zoomed, because the page disabled pinch-to-zoom on mobile.
- **The phone number couldn't be tapped.** A number in an image can't be dialled. You'd have to note it down and type it in.
- **The quote form had six required fields.** On a phone, arriving from an ad.
- **The main content took close to nine seconds to appear** on mobile. Google's recommendation is two and a half.

And the vast majority of the traffic came from mobile.

**For a product like this, the buyer usually wants to talk to a specialist.** An immediate phone call is the most valuable lead there is, and the landing page made exactly that the hardest thing to do.

## Third question: what does someone see when they check you out?

Nobody makes a big purchase decision based on an ad. They look up the company.

The company's social page hadn't had a new post in months. The ad got barely any reactions. **Anyone seeing that can fairly ask: is this company still active?**

This isn't a marketing beauty contest. On a decision worth millions, it's a question of trust.

## The lesson

**None of these are the ad's fault.** A good ad is wasted just the same if there's no measurement behind it, the landing page wasn't built for phones, and the company page has been quiet for ages.

The worst move at that point is raising the budget. More money into the same pipe, leaking the same way.

**Fix the pipe first.** Then the money.

## What can be fixed within days

The good news is that some of this isn't much work:

1. **Campaign tags on every ad link.** A few minutes in the ad settings.
2. **Allow zooming on mobile.** On most sites, a single line of code.
3. **The offer as text, not an image.** With a tappable phone number.
4. **A shorter quote form.** Name, phone, one field for the question. The rest gets covered on the call anyway.
5. **If the website can't be measured right now,** Facebook's own lead ads, with a form inside the platform, give you measurable quote requests in the meantime. It doesn't replace fixing the website, but the next campaign won't run blind.

Measurement has one prerequisite: **the tracking code may only start after the visitor consents.** That needs a consent manager and an up-to-date privacy notice, reviewed by a lawyer. It's not a step worth skipping, however urgent the campaign feels.
