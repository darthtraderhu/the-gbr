A buyer who ten years ago typed "packaging machine distributor" into Google now increasingly asks ChatGPT, Gemini or Google's AI overview: *"Who should I buy a packaging machine from if I need servicing too?"*

The answer names a few companies. **The question is whether yours is one of them.**

This is called AEO (Answer Engine Optimization): whether AI-based answer systems can quote your site accurately and as a source. It needs essentially the same signals as Google does, only more strictly. **What's vague to a search engine, an AI either misreads or leaves out.**

## What we looked at

Over the past few weeks we reviewed two company websites from the outside. Both tidy, with good photos and working product pages. To a human eye, nothing was wrong with them.

Then we looked at what a machine sees.

## Images instead of text

On one site's homepage, **not a single sentence said who the company is or what it offers.** A menu, banner images, product tiles with one-word titles and "Read more..." links.

A person gets that from the photos. A machine doesn't. **An AI assistant doesn't work from images.** If the site doesn't say in text what the company sells, to whom, with what service and since when, there's nothing for it to quote.

On the same site, the entire copy of a campaign offer was baked into an image. All a search engine learns from that is that the page has a picture on it.

**The company's strongest trust signal, official reseller status with a manufacturer, appeared only in the running text of a subpage**, two or three clicks away.

## Zero descriptions across sixty-three pages

Every page can have a short description that Google shows in the search results. On one of the sites we reviewed, **not one of its sixty-three pages had one.** So Google picked its own snippet, which on the homepage was typically the menu.

On the other site the description was often just the page name. The contact page's title and description were both simply "Contact". The product page's: the product name, nothing else. **From the search results you couldn't tell what the company did or what the product was.**

## The same page nineteen times

A sitemap is the file that tells search engines which pages a website has.

One site's sitemap held **more than sixteen thousand entries for barely eight hundred and sixty distinct pages.** The same URL nineteen times on average. Every one of those sixteen thousand entries gave the review date as its last-modified date, so the field signalled nothing.

Meanwhile, every pack size of the same product had its own near-identical page, with nothing telling the search engine which was the main version.

The other site's sitemap submitted its error page and its search results page to search engines as content.

**This isn't a disaster. But a search engine's time is finite**, and it spends that time on redundant URLs instead of real content.

## Nothing in machine-readable form

There's a standard format (structured data, JSON-LD) that lets a website say, in a machine-readable way: this is a company, here's its name, address and phone number; this is a product, here's its manufacturer and pack size; these are the frequently asked questions and their answers.

**Neither site had any.** Not on a single page.

This is the layer an AI system can work from most reliably, because it doesn't have to guess anything. It's written down.

## The FAQ nobody can find

One site had an FAQ page of about twelve hundred words. Exactly the kind of content an answer system likes to quote.

**Except it didn't have a single heading.** The questions ran on one after another as continuous text, so to a machine they weren't separate. You couldn't tell where one question started and its answer began.

And the product pages were missing exactly what a buyer would ask: what it's for, which surface, which machine, how long installation takes.

## Why the score doesn't show this

One of the sites scored 85 on Google's own SEO check. That sounds good.

**But that check looks at technical basics:** is there a page title, can the page be indexed. Not whether there's anything worth indexing.

Technically, the site could be found. **What it needed was content that gives people a reason to find it.**

## What to check on your own site

1. **Does your homepage say in a sentence or two, in text, who you are and what you offer?** Not in an image, not in a slogan.
2. **Does every important page have its own unique description?**
3. **Are your FAQs there, with a separate heading for each question?**
4. **Is your strongest trust signal** (manufacturer partnership, founding year, references) **on the homepage,** or only on a subpage?
5. **If you ask ChatGPT about your field right now,** does it mention your company, and does it describe what you do accurately?

The fifth is the most telling. Worth trying.

## What we can promise, and what we can't

**Nobody can guarantee that an AI assistant will recommend your company.** Anyone who promises that is promising something that isn't in their hands.

What we can do is make your site readable, well-structured and machine-interpretable. Unique titles and descriptions, structured data built into the template so every new page gets it automatically, a clean sitemap, content broken down question by question. **Without these, you certainly won't be quoted. With them, there's something to quote.**
