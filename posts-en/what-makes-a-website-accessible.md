Say "accessible website" and most people picture something vaguely charitable. It's more concrete than that: **there are people who can't buy on your site today**, even though they'd like to.

Not because the site is bad. Because nobody considered them when it was designed.

## Four situations that happen right now

**Someone navigating by keyboard only.** Their hand might shake, their wrist might be injured, or it's simply faster for them. They tab through the page — and nothing shows where they are. Focus indication is switched off because the designer thought it looked ugly.

**Someone using a screen reader.** The machine reads the page aloud. It reaches a product image and hears: *"image."* Then again: *"image."* No idea what they're looking at.

**Someone with low vision.** The button text is light grey on lighter grey. Enough contrast for a twenty-year-old to read in good light — not enough for a sixty-year-old to read in the evening.

**Someone on a phone, on a bus, one-handed.** The "Add to cart" button is twenty pixels tall. Three tries to hit it, two misses, then they close the site.

**In none of these cases is the problem that the site is ugly. The problem is that they can't pay.**

## This affects far more people than you'd think

When people hear "accessibility," they picture a narrow group. Most measures actually reach a **wide one**:

- Older users with declining eyesight
- Someone typing one-handed with a broken wrist
- Someone checking their phone in bright sunlight
- Someone on a slow connection
- Someone in a hurry

**And there's a side effect few people mention:** the same fixes also help search visibility. Semantic structure, image descriptions, a clean heading hierarchy — Google reads a page the same way a screen reader does. **One piece of work, two outcomes.**

## What changed in 2025

The European Accessibility Act has extended to consumer-facing online services since June 2025 — including online stores.

Exactly who it covers and what exemptions apply is a legal question, and a lawyer answers that.

**What we see from the market:** most Hungarian and Central European SME stores don't meet the technical requirements — and most don't know the topic exists.

**Same situation as GDPR before 2018.** Whoever moved early got ahead. Whoever waited until it was urgent scrambled, and paid more.

## What an assessment typically finds

Run an assessment on a typical SME site, and these are the usual findings:

**Contrast.** On buttons, secondary text, footer links. Looked fine in the design file; unreadable in reality.

**Missing image descriptions.** A store often has hundreds of images with no alt text. That alone makes the whole catalogue unusable with a screen reader.

**Focus.** Keyboard navigation doesn't show where the user is. Often switched off on purpose because "it's not pretty."

**Forms.** No real label on the field, just faint placeholder text — which disappears the moment you start typing.

**Tap target size.** The recommended minimum is 44×44 pixels. In practice it's often half that.

## Worth understanding here

**Automated checkers only catch a fraction of this.**

There are plenty of free tools that run a scan and hand you a list. Useful — but most of the real problems are things a machine can't judge.

An image description can exist and still be useless (*"img_123.jpg"*). A form can be technically valid and still untrackable with a keyboard. **A page can pass every automated test while a screen reader user still can't do anything with it.**

That's why we go through it by hand too — with a keyboard, with a screen reader, the way a real user would. Slower, and not very photogenic. **But it's the only way to find out what's actually going on.**

## How much work fixing it is

**Most items are surprisingly small.**

Contrast is a colour-value change. The focus indicator is a few lines of CSS. Enlarging a tap target, likewise. **Together, these are often done in a single day.**

Image descriptions are the most labour-intensive part, because they're written one at a time — but that's a matter of time, not complexity.

**What's genuinely a lot of work:** if the site's structure is fundamentally broken. If everything's a `<div>`, if there's no heading hierarchy, if the forms are hand-rolled buttons pretending to be inputs. At that point the fix is closer to a rebuild — and it's usually worth just doing that.

**The assessment is what decides this**, before anyone spends money in the wrong direction.
