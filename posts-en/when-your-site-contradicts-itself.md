A website isn't written all at once. The price list was updated last year, the services page two years ago, the home page hasn't changed since launch. Someone else looks after the Google profile, and yet another person runs the social page.

**Each part was true when it was written. They just don't say the same thing anymore.**

On the websites we audited in recent months, this is one of the most common problems. It doesn't show at first glance, because each page is fine on its own. It shows when someone opens two.

## What we found

**Same product, two prices.** On one site, the price of the same product differed by almost 15 percent between the price list and the product page. Both numbers were right at some point. Which one is valid today, the site didn't say.

**Three sets of opening hours.** At a local service business, the header said weekdays from morning to evening, the contact page said "24 hours", and the Google profile showed the same hours every day. Whoever calls on a weekend evening is bound to be disappointed by one of them.

**Prices "by phone only", while public elsewhere.** One site said prices were given over the phone. Meanwhile the company's social page had the full price list.

**The number that never ages.** "For X years now", "more than X years". Someone typed that number in once, and it hasn't changed since. If the founding year appears anywhere else, a visitor can do the maths and see the number is out of date.

**The expired offer.** A spring discount in autumn. A limited-time deal whose deadline passed long ago. Nothing broke; nobody took it down.

## Why it matters more than you'd think

A single inconsistency rarely decides on its own whether someone gets in touch. **But each one puts a small question mark exactly where the decision is made.**

Someone asking for a quote is trying to decide whether they can trust you. If they see two prices, they don't think an update was missed. They think **the final price will be different anyway.** If they see three sets of opening hours, they don't know when to call, and they'd rather call someone where it's clear.

And someone else reads your site too: **search engines and AI assistants.** When someone asks them what your service costs or whether you're open on Saturday, they answer from your site. If your site says two things, you can't know which one they'll quote. We wrote more about this in [the article on what an AI assistant sees on your site](/en/writing/what-chatgpt-sees-on-your-site).

## Why it happens

Because the same information lives in several places, and each one has to be updated by hand.

The price is in the price list, on the product page, in an old newsletter and in a downloadable PDF. The opening hours are in the header, the footer, on the contact page and on the Google profile. **Whoever makes a change makes it in one place, because they don't know there are three more.**

## How to check it yourself

Take a sheet of paper and write down the five things your customers look for most often: price, opening hours, contact details, service area, lead time.

Then find each of them **everywhere on the site, on your Google profile and on your social page.** Wherever they differ, decide which one is true and fix the rest.

After that, it's worth setting things up so that next time nobody has to check this by hand.
