When someone leaves a company, there's usually a list: laptop returned, access card deactivated, final payslip. What's rarely on it is the question that matters most:

**Where can they still log in?**

We don't mean their company email, which usually gets disabled. We mean everything else: the ad account, the social media page, the online shop's admin panel, the domain, the shared drive, the accounting software.

## How access spreads inside a company

Nobody plans to scatter access around. It just happens.

The new marketer gets the Facebook page password because something has to go out today. The agency gets admin rights on the Google Ads account to set up a campaign. The developer registers the hosting under their own name because it was quicker. The shared password lives in a spreadsheet five people know about.

**Every one of those decisions made sense at the time.** Two years later, nobody can put together who has access to what, and why.

## Why it matters even if nobody misuses it

Most former colleagues and partners are honest. The problem isn't necessarily intent. It's three other things.

**Accountability.** If someone changes a campaign in the ad account or edits a price in the shop, and five people share the same password, there's no way to tell who it was.

**Dependence.** If the domain or the ad account sits under a former employee's or a previous agency's name, your company's asset is in someone else's hands. While the relationship is good, nobody notices. When you need to move, or an invoice is disputed, it suddenly matters a lot.

**Account security.** An old, forgotten account that nobody monitors, with a password unchanged for years and no two-factor authentication, is exactly the entry point an attacker looks for. No disgruntled ex-colleague required: it's enough for their password to have leaked somewhere else.

## A simple check you can do today

You don't need an IT person for this. Take a sheet of paper and go through the list:

1. **Email and office accounts** (Microsoft 365, Google Workspace): does anyone who no longer works for you still have an active account? Who receives former colleagues' mail?
2. **Social pages and ad accounts** (Meta Business, Google Ads, LinkedIn): who are the admins? Do you know all of them, and do they all still work with you?
3. **Domain and hosting**: whose name is the domain registered under? Who gets the renewal notice?
4. **Website and shop admin panel**: how many admin users are there? Is there an "admin", a "test" or a former developer's name among them?
5. **Shared passwords**: is there any login that several people use with the same password?

If your answer to any of these is "I don't know", you've already found the task.

## How to put it in order

**Every account belongs to the company, not to a person.** The domain, the ad account and the hosting are owned by the company. Employees and partners get access, not ownership.

**Everyone gets their own login.** Instead of shared passwords, each person signs in with their own account. You can see who did what, and removing one person doesn't mean everyone needs a new password.

**Two-factor authentication wherever possible.** This is the step that makes a leaked password useless.

**Keep a leaver checklist.** Not just the laptop and the card, but every system the person had access to. The same goes for ending a contract with an agency or a developer.

**And review it once a year.** Access multiplies on its own. The only thing that keeps it in order is someone looking at it regularly.
