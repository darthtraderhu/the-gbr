It sounds like an odd question. You ordered the website, you paid for it, your company's name is on it. Of course it's yours.

**Except a website isn't one thing. It's at least five.** And each of them has its own owner.

## What a website is actually made of

**The domain**, the address people type into the browser. Someone registered it, it's in someone's name, and someone gets the renewal notice.

**The hosting**, where the site runs. It's an account with a provider, with an owner and a password.

**The site itself**, the code or the content management system (WordPress, for example), with all its settings and plugins.

**The admin panel**, where you edit content. Someone holds the highest level of access there too.

**Analytics and ads**: Google Analytics, Search Console, ad accounts. This is where the data builds up that shows, years back, what actually works.

If any of these sits in the developer's or a former agency's name, part of your website is theirs, whoever paid for it.

## Why it ends up this way

Not out of bad intent. The developer wanted to move fast, so they registered the domain in their own account. They put the hosting in their own reseller account because they get a discount there. They connected Google Analytics with their own Google account because it was at hand.

While the relationship is good and the developer is reachable, none of this shows. Everything works, the invoices arrive, the site runs.

## When it comes to light

When you need to change something. Because:

- the developer got busy and hasn't replied for weeks,
- you want another company to rebuild the site,
- an invoice is disputed,
- or simply nobody can reach them.

That's when it turns out the domain can't be transferred because it isn't in your account. You have no login to the hosting. Years of analytics data sit in a Google account you can't access. In the worst case the domain expires, because the renewal notice went to an address nobody reads.

**A website can be rebuilt. A domain built up over years, and your analytics history, can't.**

## Five questions you can answer today

Anyone can check these, no technical knowledge needed:

1. **Whose name is the domain in?** In the registrar's records, the owner should be your company, and the contact email an address someone at the company actually reads.
2. **Do you have your own login to the hosting?** Not through the developer's account, but one in your own name.
3. **Do you have a full backup or the source code of the site?** Ask for it and keep it at the company.
4. **Do you have admin access to the site's editing panel?** At the highest permission level, not just as an editor.
5. **Do Google Analytics, Search Console and the ad accounts belong to the company's account?** The developer should get access, but you should be the owner.

If you don't know the answer to one of them, ask your developer. A decent partner will gladly hand things over: it's simpler for them too when it's clear what belongs to whom.

## The principle

Whoever you work with, one rule is worth insisting on: **the domain, the hosting, the analytics and the ad accounts are in the company's name, and the developer gets access, not ownership.** The source code and the backups should be at the company too.

The reason is simple: **if you ever want to work with someone else, it should be a handover, not a rescue operation.**
