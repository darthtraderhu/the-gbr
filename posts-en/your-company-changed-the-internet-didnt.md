A lot changes over a company's life. It takes a new name, merges into a bigger company, moves, gets a new phone number, or simply commissions a new website.

The new site goes live and everyone relaxes. **The old addresses, profiles and pages don't disappear on their own.** They stay, and keep saying what was true years ago.

## A manufacturer we reviewed

A manufacturer we reviewed merged into a larger company a few years ago. The new company has its own website. The old company's site, however, **was still fully online more than two years later**: products, references, contact page, everything.

What an outsider saw:

- **Searching for the old company name, Google put the old site first**, not the new one.
- **Google's AI overview gave the old address and the old phone number.**
- **There were two map listings, at two different addresses.** Neither was where the company works today.
- **The old site displayed a quality certificate that had expired two years earlier.** It's the first thing a procurement manager checks.
- **The map on the contact page pointed to a third company's premises.**
- On top of that, the old site loaded analytics before consent, with an English-only cookie bar and a form without a privacy notice. In other words, **a site nobody looked after was collecting data in the company's name.**

The good news was in the same case: a third, older domain already redirected to the new site. Someone had done this work once already, just not everywhere.

## Why machines believe the old site

Because the old site has built up a history over the years. Links point to it, search engines have known it for a long time, and it matches the old company name exactly. **The new site starts from nothing by comparison**, and unless someone tells the search engine the two are the same company, it sees two separate companies.

AI assistants work the same way. When someone asks about your company, they answer from what they find. If the old details appear in more places than the new ones, **the answer will give the old ones**, confidently.

And the buyer doesn't know it's outdated. They call the old number, go to the old address, or simply decide not to start with a company that looks this confused.

## What to fix when your company changes

**1. List everywhere the company appears.** Old and new domains, Google business profiles, map apps, directories and listings, social profiles, links on partners' sites, email signatures. The list is always longer than you'd think.

**2. Don't switch the old site off. Redirect it.** Every important address on the old site should point to its matching page on the new one, not just to the homepage. This is a 301 redirect: it tells search engines to look at the new address from now on. We wrote about doing it without losing your rankings [in this article](/en/writing/new-website-without-losing-google).

**3. Don't let the old domain expire.** If it lapses, anyone can buy it and publish whatever they like under your old name. Renewing it costs little compared to that risk.

**4. Update or close your map listings.** Your Google business profile and other map services should match the company's details. If an old site has closed, mark it as closed instead of leaving it open.

**5. Review the documents published in the company's name.** Certificates, catalogues, price lists in PDF. Anything you don't keep current, take down, or mark with the period it was valid for.

**6. Say on the new site that the old company is you.** One sentence on your About page ("Since 2024, the former X Ltd. is part of our company") helps both buyers and machines connect the two. In the structured data for machines this can be marked explicitly too.

This isn't only about mergers. **You need the same list when you move, rename, change your phone number**, and every time a new website replaces an old one.

## Check it yourself in five minutes

Search Google for the company's **old name** and **old phone number**. Look at what comes up first and what the map result says.

Then ask an AI assistant: "Where is [company name] based, and what number can I reach them on?" If the answer contains old details, now you know where they come from.
